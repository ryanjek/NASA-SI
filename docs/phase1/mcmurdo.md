# McMurdo Dry Valleys

The Dry Valleys are not one analog. The useful distinction is between high, no-thaw valleys, where ground ice exchanges with the air as vapor, and lower valleys, where summer temperatures go above freezing and water moves salts and sediment. Coordinates, claims, and citations are in `data/phase1/sites.csv` and `data/phase1/processes.csv`.

## University Valley

Marinova et al. (2011) place University Valley in the Quartermain Mountains at 77°51.8′S, 160°43′E, about 1730 m, next to the polar plateau. Air temperature stays below freezing, mean annual temperature is about −25°C, and humidity is about 50%. The surface can still warm above freezing in the sun. Dry permafrost, soil that stays frozen but is not ice-cemented, overlies ice-cemented ground. The ice is at the surface at the valley head and deeper than 70 cm at the mouth. A station about a third of the way down recorded 42 cm.

Williams et al. (2018) model two opposing controls. Thin seasonal snow can strongly slow ice-table loss. Near-surface NaCl, CaCl2, or perchlorate, without that snow, slightly increases loss because the salt holds vapor. That paper prints 77.868°S, 163.758°E. The latitude matches. The longitude does not, and it is not the Quartermain site beside Beacon Valley. The catalog keeps the Marinova coordinate and records the conflict.

Tamppari et al. (2012) compared University Valley with lower Taylor Valley for Phoenix. In University Valley, salts, clay-sized material, and biomass stay near the surface, which they read as little downward movement of water. They also report microorganisms in an ultraxerous soil and conclude that the Phoenix site is drier still. Biomass and salt profiles are reference-only features. Days above freezing and humidity are the global screens, and they do not measure ice-table depth.

## Central Beacon Valley

Liu et al. (2018) instrumented one polygon at 77°49′S, 160°39′E. Massive ice with about 10 wt% debris is 30–50 cm down, under 0.30 m of sublimation residue. Liu et al. (2015) describe the valley floor near 1350 m and give the valley as 77°49′S, 161°39′E. The catalog uses the 2018 study site, not a valley-wide center.

The age of that ice is contested. Sugden et al. (1995) and Marchant et al. (2002) interpret volcanic ash in the till as evidence that the ice has lasted at least 8.1 million years, and that polygon centers have not churned the ash. Schorghofer (2005) shows that vapor diffusion into a dry atmosphere is hard to reconcile with an 8 million year age unless humidity slows the loss, and that a 5°C cooling would stop retreat. Liu et al. (2015) add episodic snow, get a 200 ka mean loss near 0.09 mm/yr, and argue that this rate allows ice older than 1 million years. Liu et al. (2018) say the ice has been sublimating for at least the past 200 ka. That is a minimum duration of loss, not a maximum age.

Polygons are not diagnostic. Beacon Valley polygons overlie massive ice. University Valley also has polygons, and the ice there is ice-cemented ground rather than the same massive body. A later search can map patterned ground. It cannot assign the ice type from the pattern.

## Victoria Valley and lower Taylor Valley

Hagedorn et al. (2007) studied ice-cemented soil at about 450 m in Victoria Valley. The ice boundary is 0.22 m down. Their diffusion model loses about 0.22 mm of ice per year, which is too fast to leave ice that shallow on a surface they treat as about 10 ka old. Summer snow reverses the vapor gradient, and they observed snowmelt recharge. No pit coordinate was printed, so the site is unlocated. It is a contrast, not a second University Valley: summer air temperature reached 10°C in their record.

Lower Taylor Valley is the wetter end of the Tamppari comparison: more evidence of water transport, aqueous alteration of mica, and higher biomass. It is stored so that "anywhere in the Dry Valleys" is not treated as the Phoenix analog. No soil-pit coordinate was in the opened abstract.

## Brines that are not dry permafrost

Don Juan Pond, in the South Fork of upper Wright Valley, is a CaCl2 brine. Dickson et al. (2013) find near-surface water-track flow and deliquescence, plus small melt inputs, and they did not observe deep groundwater discharge in the season they imaged. They connect the pond to Martian recurring slope lineae and to ancient chloride deposits. Their camera coordinate, 77.563°S, 161.256°E, is a vantage point on the Dais, so the pond itself is unlocated. The pond is a search seed for brine, not for the Phoenix ice table.

Blood Falls is an iron-rich outflow from a sulfate-rich brine under Taylor Glacier. Mikucki et al. (2009) describe a microbial sulfur cycle that uses ferric iron, in a brine isolated from photosynthesis. The paper's stated comparison is organic-starved oceans. The site is kept so it is not folded into the dry-permafrost claim, and it is not a search seed.

## What a later search can and cannot use

Public layers can screen elevation, summer warmth, days above freezing, humidity, and coarse snow persistence. They cannot see ice-table depth, ice type, salt profiles, or biomass. MODIS snow is about 500 m and does not resolve the thin snow in the University Valley model. NASA POWER is a model grid, not an LTER station. The McMurdo LTER portal is cataloged only as reference data.
